Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a joint disease in which cartilage and other joint tissues change and break down over time. It commonly affects the hands, knees, hips, neck, and lower back.

What is osteoarthritis?

OA affects the entire joint, including cartilage, bone, ligaments, tendons, and the joint lining. It is not simply an unavoidable result of aging. Symptoms and their effect on daily life vary widely, and joint changes usually develop gradually.

Common symptoms

  • Pain during or after joint use
  • Stiffness after rest, usually lasting less than 30 minutes
  • Swelling around a joint, especially after activity
  • Reduced motion or a feeling that a joint is unstable
  • Grinding, cracking, or other sounds with movement
  • Difficulty walking, climbing stairs, gripping objects, or completing daily activities

How OA is evaluated

There is no single test for osteoarthritis. Evaluation includes the location and pattern of symptoms, a physical examination, and assessment of how the joint functions. X-rays may help evaluate joint-space changes, bone remodeling, or bone spurs. Blood tests or joint-fluid analysis may be used when another cause—such as inflammatory arthritis, gout, or infection—needs to be considered.

Treatment and joint function

Treatment aims to reduce pain and improve mobility and function. A personalized plan may include education, strengthening and low-impact exercise, physical or occupational therapy, weight management when appropriate, braces or assistive devices, topical or oral medicines, and selected joint injections. When symptoms and joint damage remain severe despite nonsurgical care, an orthopedic evaluation may be appropriate.

Movement matters: An individualized exercise plan can improve strength, flexibility, endurance, and joint function. Begin gradually and discuss a safe program with a clinician or physical therapist.
Seek prompt care: Sudden severe joint pain, marked warmth or swelling, inability to bear weight, fever, or symptoms following a significant injury may indicate a problem other than routine OA.
