Rheumatoid Arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that causes joint inflammation. Early evaluation and appropriate treatment can help control symptoms and reduce the risk of lasting joint damage.

What is rheumatoid arthritis?

In RA, the immune system mistakenly attacks the body’s own tissues. The condition most often affects joints and may also involve the eyes, lungs, heart, skin, or other organs. Symptoms often affect similar joints on both sides of the body.

Common symptoms

  • Joint pain, tenderness, swelling, or warmth
  • Morning stiffness lasting longer than 30 minutes
  • Difficulty using the hands or completing daily activities
  • Fatigue, reduced energy, or occasional low-grade fever

How RA is evaluated

There is no single test that confirms RA in every patient. Evaluation may include a medical history, joint examination, inflammation tests, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibody testing, and imaging such as X-rays or ultrasound. Some people with RA have normal antibody results, so findings must be considered together.

Treatment and monitoring

Treatment is personalized and may include disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), biologic or targeted medicines, short-term symptom control, physical or occupational therapy, and regular safety monitoring. The goals are to control inflammation, preserve function, and prevent or slow joint damage.

Seek prompt care: A joint that becomes suddenly very painful, hot, and swollen—especially with fever—may have an infection or another urgent cause.
